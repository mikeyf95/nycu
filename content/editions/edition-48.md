# Edition 48 - NYCU
**Period:** 15th - 28th August 2026
**Status:** Final

## Opening

Nashville lost its brightest star, Dolly Parton, on the 25th of August 2026; she was 80. That same week the city hosted ILTACON, although it’s hard to pretend that anything even a fraction as significant happened at that conference. While tributes sang out across Music City, ILTACON carried on a few miles away, as these things do.

Google chose the middle of the conference to enter the legal market. The trade press spent four days covering ILTACON, although the more durable developments happened elsewhere.

Harvey and Thomson Reuters each stopped renting frontier intelligence and started running models they had built themselves, four days apart. Meanwhile, the SRA published a warning notice making clear to solicitors that accountability does not transfer to a tool. Research published at about the same time suggests that firms have written the policies but mostly lack the systems needed to check whether anyone follows them.

Each of these is a question about ownership. Vendors are moving towards models trained on individual firms' work, raising questions about who owns the resulting intelligence rather than simply who licenses the software. Edition 44 of NYCU asked how much of our capability we control and how much we rent; the answer now looks less comfortable. Supervision is the same question asked about accountability: most firms still appear unable to produce the evidence a regulator would want to see. And the Google launch is the third form of it, which is who owns the interface a lawyer works in all day.

### Learning On the Job

[Harvey - Post-Training Update: Harvey Tenet](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet) | [Artificial Lawyer - TR Launches Thomson 1.0, Its Own LLM](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/08/24/tr-launches-thomson-1-0-its-own-llm/) | [Artificial Lawyer - Avvoka Partners With Harvey, Launches Curate](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/08/20/avvoka-partners-with-harvey-launches-curate-for-templates/) | [The American Lawyer - Training AI Tools on Client Data? Talk to Clients First](https://www.law.com/americanlawyer/2026/07/23/training-ai-tools-on-client-data-talk-to-clients-first-/) | [Simon Willison - Anthropic's Best AI Model Struggles to Attract Users as Cheaper Tools Thrive](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/23/anthropics-best-ai-model-struggles-to-attract-users-as-cheaper-t/)

- **What:** On 20 August Harvey published Tenet, its first post-trained model, using Kimi K3, the open-weight model from Chinese lab Moonshot AI, as its base. Harvey says no customer data was used. Four days later Thomson Reuters launched Thomson, its first proprietary LLM, also built on an open-source foundation, with a stated $40 million in training spend. What Harvey says comes next may matter more. Co-founder Gabe Pereyra told Business Insider that firms could train Tenet on their institutional knowledge, leaving two Harvey customers with different models. Other launches point the same way. Draftwise's Legal Ontology aims to capture judgment in the relationships between a firm's documents. Avvoka's Curate turns transaction documents into templates, retaining Avvoka as the source of approved wording while Claude, Copilot or Harvey drafts. The obstacle is not new. The American Lawyer reported in July that outside counsel guidelines are becoming more permissive on some AI uses, although comfort levels vary. In-house counsel object both to data leakage and to their material improving work for competitors. Law firm AI leaders said questions about vendor agreements prohibiting training on client data are increasingly appearing in outside counsel guidelines. Some clients require sensitive material to be excluded or redacted from AI tools regardless of existing confidentiality provisions. SmartEsq's Esther Chiang put the precedent problem plainly: asked about market terms, a firm will look across its holdings regardless of whose matter the wording came from. AI makes that practice permanent and scalable. The Association of Corporate Counsel already publishes sample outside-counsel AI guidelines, so clients have a template available.

- **So what:** Treat the model performance claims as marketing pending independent testing. Harvey reports Tenet as state of the art on LAB Contracts and second across the broader Legal Agent Bench results, but LAB is Harvey's own benchmark and the model is a research preview with no published weights, model card or API endpoint. Harvey has released the method rather than the model itself. Both companies appear to have concluded that renting frontier intelligence by the API call does not work at the volume agentic products need, and both chose a cheap open-weight base instead. The Financial Times reported on 23 August that Anthropic's most expensive model has plateaued at around 11% of spend on its own tools, on Ramp card data from 70,000 companies. Rudy DeFelice told Legal IT Insider on the 21st that the token subsidy era has ended. Edition 44 asked how much capability we rent from foundation model providers; the vendors' choices suggest they are trying to reduce that dependence. This creates a contractual issue that no supplier of a firm-specific model has addressed this month. A model post-trained on a firm's matter history is different from a licence because it is built from material that clients are increasingly restricting, though nobody has published a reliable figure for how many. If a fork trained partly on work for one client improves output on a matter for another, whether that is permitted will depend on the wording. Most firms have not audited their documents for this. We probably do not know our own position. The first action should therefore be a count: how many of our live engagement terms and client outside counsel guidelines carry an AI training restriction, how many require disclosure of tools used, and how many predate the point where any of this was contemplated. That work is worth doing without a reliable market figure. Exit terms should address the weights as well as the licence, because a firm-specific model cannot be handed back in the way a subscription can be cancelled. Chiang's precedent question may be harder to answer because it goes beyond AI. Firms have always mined their own back catalogue for market terms; AI makes that process systematic, continuous and visible to a client who asks. Edition 47's consolidation piece argued that buyers were paying for talent and proprietary data because those are the things tooling cannot generate. The same issue now appears in product roadmaps and contract terms.

### The Honour System

[SRA - Solicitors cautioned on safe and responsible use of AI](https://www.sra.org.uk/news/news/releases/responsible-use-ai/) | [Legal Futures - SRA warns profession over AI misuse amidst investigations](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/sra-warns-profession-over-ai-misuse-amidst-investigations) | [Legaltech Hub - For Europe's Law Firms, the Challenge Isn't Accessing AI, It's Operationalizing It](https://www.legaltechnologyhub.com/contents/for-europes-law-firms-the-challenge-isnt-accessing-ai-its-operationalizing-it/)

- **What:** On 17 August the SRA published a warning notice on AI misuse. It requires solicitors to check material put before the court, supervise work done for clients and remain accountable for work produced with AI. Legal Futures reported that the SRA received 42 reports of possible AI misuse between July 2025 and July 2026. Several investigations are still running, covering inaccurate citations, supervision failures and confidentiality. The 42 figure is the number of reports received, not findings made, and the SRA has not said how many led to investigations. It is also a floor because it captures only matters someone reported. The notice assumes that a firm asked about supervision could show what that supervision involved. The available evidence suggests that many firms could not. Legaltech Hub and Lexpo published their European Law Firms and Legal AI 2026 survey on 18 August. Firms rated their AI maturity at an average of 6.7 out of ten and most have an AI leader, formal policies and governance committees. But LTH concludes that governance structures have matured far faster than governance technology: strikingly few firms have systems to monitor usage, evaluate outputs or measure effectiveness at scale.

- **So what:** The LTH findings still need some caution: maturity is self-rated, the article does not publish a sample size and the fieldwork was conducted in spring 2026. But the fuller findings make the central gap clearer, not weaker. Firms have created leadership roles, policies and committees faster than they have built the monitoring, evaluation and measurement systems needed to make those arrangements work. That is where the SRA notice lands. A supervision policy is not enough if a firm cannot show what happened on a particular matter. It needs to reconstruct how the tool was used, how the output was assessed and who approved the work. LTH suggests that few firms can do that consistently. The answer is not simply more human checking. If a firm has no logging or evaluation system, manual review becomes its only visible control and has to compensate for uncertainty elsewhere. Better records and clear competency standards allow review to be targeted at higher-risk work rather than applied indiscriminately. Edition 47 made a related point about competency frameworks following the BARBRI research. For us, AI Summer's per-task logging provides one part of that evidence. The pod feedback serves another purpose. It should show not only whether people use the tool, but whether they can recognise unreliable output and respond appropriately. Taken together, the logging and feedback begin to evidence supervision rather than merely assert it.

### Right of Way

[Artificial Lawyer - Gemini for Legal and the Battle for Centrality](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/08/26/gemini-for-legal-and-the-battle-for-centrality/) | [LawSites - DeepJudge Releases an Open Protocol for Passing Users and Their Context Between AI Products](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/08/deepjudge-releases-an-open-protocol-for-passing-users-and-their-context-between-ai-products-with-harvey-and-thomson-reuters-on-board.html) | [Legal IT Insider - Google Cloud unveils Gemini Enterprise for Legal](https://legaltechnology.com/google-cloud-unveils-gemini-enterprise-for-legal/)

- **What:** Google Cloud launched Gemini Enterprise for Legal on 25 August, mid-ILTACON. Weil contributed to its development, and Legora joined as a launch partner. Artificial Lawyer's read was that the significant thing is not the product but the fight it joins: the platforms all want to be the place a lawyer starts their day. That is a reasonable description of the rest of the fortnight. Harvey shipped Harvey II with persistent memory and per-matter context. LexisNexis added an agentic harness to Protégé so multiple tools run inside one workflow. Each is an argument for consolidating on a single front door. At the same time the market spent the last two weeks building corridors between the doors. DeepJudge published the Agent Handoff Protocol on 13 August, just before this window, with Harvey and Thomson Reuters as first adopters. AHP is deliberately complementary to MCP rather than a replacement: MCP lets systems exchange information and perform actions, while AHP transfers the user together with the context needed to continue a task, meaning the objective, the supporting materials, the conversation history and the prior work. Harvey's implementation was due in beta this month and Thomson Reuters said it will support it in CoCounsel for Legal without giving timing. Yannic Kilcher was clear that the specification documents what DeepJudge has already built with partners rather than something aspirational, that it is published openly, and that nothing about it is legal-specific. There were also more integration announcements than usual. Thomson Reuters and iManage deepened their partnership with Model Context Protocol support, while Avvoka connected to Harvey through MCP. Legora announced integrations with DeepJudge, Avvoka, Intapp and NetDocuments inside nine days. LawToolBox published an MCP server connecting M365 matter data to Claude.

- **So what:** Vendors are pursuing both consolidation and interoperability. Google, Harvey and LexisNexis are trying to become the main interface, while AHP and the integration announcements assume firms will continue to use more than one platform. Supporting both approaches makes sense for vendors, but leaves buyers with a less obvious architecture choice. The portability case is stronger on the current evidence, with one caveat: three adopters do not make a standard. Harvey is shipping AHP, Thomson Reuters has committed to supporting it without giving a date, and DeepJudge is itself an adopter. The protocol has not yet been tested by anyone with an incentive to break it. MCP is further along and is doing more actual work in this fortnight's announcements than AHP is. If we are going to make a bet, MCP support is the one to write into requirements now, with AHP as a question we ask rather than a requirement we impose. The LTH survey adds a detail that cuts against most of the launch messaging. It found adoption still concentrated in research, drafting and summarisation, with due diligence, translation and search lagging, and workflow automation and agentic systems at the early-interest stage. This is also the fortnight in which NetDocuments launched tabular review for M&A diligence and every platform pushed agentic workflows. Vendors are moving ahead of present firm adoption. That may prove well judged, but the survey cannot tell us whether demand will follow.

### Policy, Courts and Governance

- **[ACC sample AI guidelines for outside counsel](https://www.acc.com/resource-library/sample-artificial-intelligence-ai-guidelines-outside-counsel):** The template referenced in the first dive, covering disclosure, data security, accuracy and performance. Worth reading as the thing a client may already be about to send us.
- **[SRA cautions the profession on safe and responsible use of AI](https://www.sra.org.uk/news/news/releases/responsible-use-ai/):** The warning notice itself, 17 August. Short, and worth reading in the original before anyone briefs on it. The three obligations it names are checking, supervising and remaining accountable.
- **[SRA warns profession over AI misuse amidst investigations](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/sra-warns-profession-over-ai-misuse-amidst-investigations):** Legal Futures has the number the SRA release does not lead on: 42 reports in a year and several live investigations. Note that 42 is reports received, not findings made, and the SRA has not said how many became investigations.
- **[Damien Charlotin's AI Hallucination Cases database](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/):** 1,963 cases as of 27 August, against 1,870 on 11 August in Edition 47. The composition is the underreported part: 1,127 involve pro se litigants and 784 involve lawyers, so the story the trade press tells about sanctioned solicitors describes a minority of the dataset. 62 UK cases.
- **[New Mexico Supreme Court removes a lawyer from a case over AI errors](https://www.santafenewmexican.com/news/local_news/new-mexico-high-court-blasts-lawyer-takes-him-off-case-for-ai-errors-in-brief/article_a162daf7-95a4-4010-a017-dedcbdb68b0b.html):** 21 August. Contempt, removal from the case, $5,000, briefs stricken, the appeal restarted and a bar referral. One of the heaviest outcomes recorded so far, and the aggravating pattern is the familiar one.
- **[Fabricated citations from a purpose-built legal AI tool](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-08-19/ai-hallucinations-case-law-state-farm-la-county-fire-dispute):** Defence counsel identified seven fabricated citations produced by the tool Irys, apologised and filed corrections. Worth keeping to hand for anyone who assumes the risk sits only with consumer chatbots.
- **[A Lawyer for AI Agents](https://www.artificiallawyer.com/2026/08/20/a-lawyer-for-ai-agents/):** Charlotin arguing for human legal representation of agents, opening with the observation that most major labs have now reported agents breaking out of sandboxes during training, and that none of the agents escalated what they were doing. The proposal is speculative; the observation underneath it is not.

### Capability and People
